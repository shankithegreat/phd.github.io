Quantization & Low-Bit Inference

Storing and computing with 8, 4, or even fewer bits per weight so that large models fit in memory and decode fast enough to be useful.

Foundation

What Is It?

Quantization replaces the high-precision numbers inside a trained network with a coarser numeric representation — typically mapping a block of 16-bit floating-point weights onto a small integer grid plus a scale factor, so each weight costs 8, 4, or fewer bits to store. Dequantization reverses the mapping approximately: the value you get back is the nearest grid point, not the original. Everything interesting about the field follows from managing that rounding error where it matters and ignoring it where it does not.

The reason this dominates deployment work is a hardware fact about autoregressive decoding. Generating one token requires reading essentially every weight in the model from memory, but performs only a single vector-matrix product per layer — an arithmetic intensity of roughly one operation per parameter loaded. On a modern accelerator, arithmetic throughput exceeds memory bandwidth by a factor of a few hundred, so single-stream decoding sits idle waiting on HBM. Decoding is memory-bandwidth bound, and halving the bytes per weight roughly halves the time per token. Prefill, which processes many tokens at once, is compute bound and behaves quite differently.

Simple Analogy

Think of shipping a library. The books are the weights and the truck is your memory bus. If you print every book on thick archival paper, only a few fit per trip and the truck is the bottleneck — the reading room stands empty waiting for deliveries. Print the same text on thin paper at slightly smaller type and four times as many books arrive per trip. Almost every reader notices nothing. But a handful of pages are diagrams with fine detail, and shrinking those ruins them — so you keep exactly those few at full size. That selective treatment is what modern quantization is really about.

There is a second, equally practical motivation: capacity. A 70B-parameter model needs roughly 140 GB in 16-bit precision and will not fit on a single 80 GB accelerator, but at 4 bits it lands near 35 GB and fits comfortably with room for a KV cache. Quantization is therefore the difference between a multi-GPU deployment and a single card, or between a cloud API and a model running on a laptop or phone. The trade-off is accuracy, and the central research question is where the accuracy actually goes as the bit-width falls.

Motivation

Why It Matters

Almost every property people care about in a deployed model — cost, latency, context length, privacy, carbon footprint — is downstream of how many bytes the weights and activations occupy.

  • ⚡ Latency is bytes, not FLOPs: Because decoding is bandwidth bound, moving from 16-bit to 4-bit weights typically yields a multi-fold speedup in tokens per second for single-stream generation, without touching the architecture or retraining anything.
  • 💾 It decides what fits: Low-bit weights are what let a capable model run on one accelerator, a consumer GPU, or an edge device. Every on-device assistant shipping today depends on aggressive quantization somewhere in the stack.
  • 💰 Serving throughput and cost: Smaller weights free HBM for a larger KV cache, which means bigger batches and more concurrent sequences per GPU. The cost per million tokens often improves more than the raw speedup suggests.
  • 🔧 It democratises research: QLoRA-style 4-bit base models with low-rank adapters made fine-tuning large models feasible on a single GPU, which materially widened who can do experimental work on models of that scale.
  • 🧪 It is a probe of redundancy: That transformers tolerate 4-bit weights but break on a handful of outlier channels tells us something real about how information is distributed in these networks — a genuine scientific question, not just an engineering trick.
Mechanism

How It Works

A quantizer is defined by three choices: the numeric format, the granularity at which scales are shared, and the procedure used to pick the rounding. Post-training quantization (PTQ) makes these choices from a small calibration set in minutes to hours; quantization-aware training (QAT) instead simulates the quantizer during training with a straight-through estimator so the weights learn to be robust to it. PTQ dominates in practice for large language models simply because retraining them is expensive.

Low-Bit Kernels

Calibration and the outlier problem: A few hundred sequences of representative text are pushed through the model to record the dynamic range of every weight matrix and activation tensor. This immediately exposes the defining pathology of large transformers: emergent outlier features. Above roughly the 6B-parameter scale, a small number of hidden dimensions — often only a handful of channels, consistent across tokens and layers — carry activation magnitudes one or two orders of magnitude larger than everything else. A single per-tensor scale stretched to cover them leaves almost no resolution for the other 99.9% of values, which is why naive INT8 quantization of a large model can collapse entirely. LLM.int8() (Dettmers et al., 2022) solved this with mixed-precision decomposition: detect the outlier channels and compute those in 16-bit while the rest of the matrix multiplication runs in INT8.

Granularity and error-aware rounding: Sharing one scale per tensor is cheapest but least accurate; per-channel scales are standard; group-wise schemes with a scale per contiguous block of 64 or 128 weights are the workhorse of 4-bit methods, costing a small overhead in effective bits per weight. On top of granularity, the rounding itself can be optimised. GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) quantizes a layer column by column and, after each step, updates the remaining full-precision weights to compensate for the error just introduced, using second-order information from an approximate Hessian estimated on the calibration data. AWQ (Lin et al., 2023) takes a different route: it uses activation statistics to identify the salient weight channels — those multiplied by large activations — and scales them up before quantization so they land on a finer effective grid. SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023) attacks the same asymmetry from the activation side, migrating the outlier magnitude out of activations and into the weights with a per-channel rescaling that is mathematically equivalent for the layer output, making both tensors easy to quantize to INT8.

Formats, the KV cache, and kernels: The numeric format matters as much as the algorithm. INT8 is well supported and nearly lossless with the outlier fix; INT4 is the sweet spot for weight-only quantization; FP8 with its exponent bits handles wide dynamic ranges more gracefully and has native support on recent datacentre hardware. NF4, the 4-bit NormalFloat introduced with QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023), places its grid points to be information-theoretically well matched to normally distributed weights and pairs it with double quantization of the scales themselves. Going further, BitNet b1.58 (Ma et al., 2024) trains from scratch with ternary weights in the set of minus one, zero, and one — roughly 1.58 bits — arguing that low precision must be built in rather than retrofitted. Beyond weights, long-context serving makes the KV cache the dominant memory consumer, so quantizing keys and values to 8 or 4 bits (usually per-token or per-head, with keys more sensitive than values) is now routine. None of this delivers speed without kernels: a low-bit format only pays off when a fused kernel dequantizes inside the GEMM loop on registers, and support for a given format, group size, and hardware generation is uneven.

Techniques

Key Techniques

Mixed-Precision Decomposition

Split each matrix multiply into an INT8 path for the bulk of channels and a 16-bit path for detected outlier dimensions. Preserves accuracy at 8 bits but the irregular split complicates kernels and limits achievable speedup.

Second-Order Weight Rounding

Quantize greedily while compensating remaining weights using approximate Hessian information from calibration data, as in GPTQ. Layer-wise, needs no retraining, and reaches usable 4-bit accuracy in hours for very large models.

Activation-Aware Scaling

Use activation magnitudes to find salient weight channels and pre-scale them so rounding hurts them less, as in AWQ. Calibration-light and robust across domains because it depends on which channels matter, not on exact activation values.

Outlier Migration

Rescale channels to shift dynamic range from activations into weights, keeping the layer output mathematically unchanged, as in SmoothQuant. Enables genuine INT8 activation-times-weight compute rather than weight-only compression.

4-Bit Base plus Adapters

Freeze a 4-bit NF4 base model and train low-rank adapters in higher precision, gradients flowing through the frozen quantized weights. The QLoRA recipe makes single-GPU fine-tuning of large models practical.

Low-Bit Training & QAT

Simulate the quantizer during training so weights adapt to it, up to ternary and binary regimes. Recovers accuracy that PTQ cannot, but costs a full training run and needs specialised kernels to realise the benefit.

Challenges

Challenges

📉

Accuracy Cliffs Perplexity Hides

A 4-bit model can lose almost nothing in perplexity yet degrade sharply on multi-step reasoning, code, long-context retrieval, and rare languages, where a single early error compounds. Reporting only perplexity systematically understates the damage.

🎲

Calibration Sensitivity

PTQ methods depend on a small calibration set, and a mismatch between that text and the deployment distribution quietly costs accuracy. Results can shift with sample count, sequence length, and random seed, which makes published comparisons fragile.

🧱

Kernel & Hardware Dependence

A format is only as fast as its kernel on your specific accelerator. Exotic group sizes and non-uniform grids often run slower than plainer schemes, and portability across GPU generations, NPUs, and CPUs is far from guaranteed.

🧬

Outliers Are Not Understood

We can detect and route around emergent outlier features, but why training concentrates so much signal into a few channels — and whether architectural changes could avoid them entirely — remains open, so mitigations stay largely empirical.

    QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs
    Dettmers et al., 2023 — Introduces the 4-bit NormalFloat format, double quantization, and paged optimizers to fine-tune large models on a single GPU.
Looking Ahead

Future Directions

  • Native low-bit pretraining: If ternary or FP4 training can be made stable at frontier scale, the retrofit problem disappears — the model is simply born cheap to serve, and hardware can be designed around the format rather than the reverse.
  • Quantization for long context: As sequences stretch into the hundreds of thousands of tokens, the KV cache dominates memory. Combining low-bit caches with eviction, sharing, and attention-sparsity methods is the practical frontier for long-context serving.
  • Reasoning-faithful compression: Better objectives and evaluations that target where quantization actually hurts — long chains of reasoning, agentic tool use, and low-resource languages — instead of optimising a perplexity number that is insensitive to those failures.
  • Co-design across the stack: Joint search over architecture, sparsity, bit-width, and kernel implementation for a specific accelerator, replacing today's hand-tuned pipeline of independent, sequentially applied compression steps.
