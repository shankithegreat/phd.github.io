📖 Definition

What Is LLM Reasoning & Chain-of-Thought?

LLM Reasoning and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) encompasses a family of techniques that enable large language models to perform complex multi-step reasoning by generating intermediate thinking steps before arriving at a final answer. Rather than jumping directly from question to answer in a single forward pass, CoT models decompose problems into a sequence of logical steps — much like a student showing their work on an exam.

The breakthrough discovery was surprisingly simple: adding the phrase "Let's think step by step" to a prompt could dramatically improve an LLM's performance on math, logic, and common-sense reasoning tasks. This phenomenon, first demonstrated by Wei et al. in 2022, revealed that LLMs possessed latent reasoning capabilities that were unlocked simply by asking the model to articulate its thought process. The act of generating intermediate steps allows the model to break complex problems into manageable sub-problems, carry intermediate results forward, and catch potential errors along the way.

Modern reasoning systems go far beyond simple prompting. o1-style reasoning models are trained with reinforcement learning to generate long internal reasoning chains before responding. Tree-of-Thoughts explores multiple reasoning paths in parallel and selects the most promising one. Process Reward Models evaluate the quality of each reasoning step, not just the final answer. Together, these advances are transforming LLMs from sophisticated autocomplete systems into machines capable of genuine multi-step problem solving — tackling mathematical proofs, complex coding challenges, and scientific reasoning that were previously thought impossible for neural networks.

🎯 Importance

Why It Matters

Reasoning is arguably the most important capability gap between current AI and human-level intelligence. Closing this gap doesn't just improve benchmark scores — it fundamentally expands the types of problems AI can reliably solve.

  • 📐 Massive accuracy improvements: Chain-of-Thought prompting improves accuracy 2-5x on complex reasoning tasks like math word problems, multi-hop question answering, and logical deduction. On the GSM8K math benchmark, CoT boosted GPT-3's accuracy from 18% to 57% — without any retraining.
  • 🧩 Unlocking new problem classes: Without explicit reasoning, LLMs fail at problems requiring more than ~2 logical steps. CoT and its successors enable tackling competition mathematics, formal logic, multi-step planning, and complex code debugging — problem classes previously thought impossible for neural networks.
  • 🔍 Interpretability and trust: When a model shows its reasoning steps, users can understand why it reached a particular conclusion, identify flawed logic, and build appropriate trust. This transparency is essential for high-stakes applications in medicine, law, and engineering where blind trust in AI outputs is unacceptable.
  • 🔄 Self-correction capability: Models that reason step-by-step can catch their own errors mid-reasoning. By verifying each step against the problem constraints, they can backtrack from incorrect paths — a capability that one-shot answering fundamentally lacks.
  • 🏗️ Foundation for agentic AI: Complex AI agents that plan, execute, and adapt require multi-step reasoning as a core capability. Every agentic system — from coding assistants to research agents — depends on the model's ability to decompose goals into sub-goals and reason about action sequences.
Real-World Analogy

Chain-of-Thought reasoning is exactly like showing your work in a math exam. Imagine two students taking a calculus test. Student A glances at each problem and writes down a final answer immediately — sometimes getting it right through intuition, but often making errors on multi-step problems with no way to catch them. Student B writes down each step: identifying the formula needed, substituting values, simplifying the expression, checking units, and arriving at the answer methodically. Student B not only gets more answers right, but when they do make an error, they (or their teacher) can pinpoint exactly where the reasoning went wrong and correct it. The exam paper itself becomes a debugging tool. That's precisely what CoT does for LLMs — the generated reasoning trace serves as both a computation scaffold and an error-checking mechanism.

The reasoning pipeline starts with the problem input and an implicit or explicit instruction to think step by step. The model then generates reasoning steps — a sequence of intermediate computations, observations, and conclusions that form a logical chain from question to answer. Each step builds on previous ones, carrying forward intermediate results and narrowing the solution space.

Step verification is where modern systems diverge from naive CoT. Process Reward Models (PRMs) evaluate each reasoning step independently, scoring its correctness and relevance. Unlike Outcome Reward Models that only judge the final answer, PRMs can catch errors early — identifying the specific step where reasoning went wrong. This mirrors how a math teacher checks each line of a proof, not just the final result.

When a problem has multiple valid approaches, the system can branch into parallel reasoning paths — this is the core idea of Tree-of-Thoughts. Different paths explore different strategies (algebraic vs. geometric, brute force vs. elegant), and the system evaluates which branches are most promising. Self-Consistency takes a simpler approach: generate multiple independent reasoning chains and take a majority vote on the final answer, leveraging the statistical insight that correct reasoning paths are more likely to agree.

Finally, the results are aggregated — through voting, reward-weighted selection, or learned aggregation — to produce a high-confidence final answer. The entire reasoning trace is available for inspection, making the process transparent and debuggable.

🔧 Techniques

Key Techniques

Chain-of-Thought Prompting

The foundational technique: providing few-shot examples with explicit reasoning steps, or simply adding "Let's think step by step." Unlocks latent reasoning in LLMs with zero additional training cost.

Tree-of-Thoughts

Extends linear CoT into a branching tree structure where the model explores multiple reasoning paths, evaluates intermediate states, and uses search algorithms (BFS/DFS) to navigate the reasoning space systematically.

Self-Consistency

Generates multiple independent reasoning chains for the same problem and selects the most common final answer through majority voting. Simple yet remarkably effective — the wisdom of crowds, applied to reasoning traces.

Process Reward Models

Trained to evaluate the correctness of each individual reasoning step rather than just the final answer. Enables fine-grained credit assignment and early error detection, dramatically improving reliability on complex problems.

STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner)

A self-improvement loop where the model generates rationales, keeps those that lead to correct answers, and fine-tunes on its own successful reasoning traces. Bootstraps reasoning capability without human-written rationales.

Verification Chains

After generating a solution, the model generates a separate verification chain that checks each step of the original reasoning. This dual-process approach mimics human deliberative thinking — solving, then double-checking.

o1-Style Reasoning

Models trained with RL to generate long internal reasoning traces (hidden from the user) before responding. Trades inference compute for accuracy by "thinking longer" on harder problems, achieving breakthrough results on math and coding.

Scratchpad Reasoning

Providing the model with an explicit "scratchpad" workspace to perform intermediate computations. Particularly effective for arithmetic, symbolic manipulation, and tasks requiring working memory beyond the model's implicit capacity.

🚧 Challenges

Open Challenges

🎭

Faithfulness of Reasoning

Models may generate plausible-looking reasoning that doesn't actually reflect their internal computation. The chain-of-thought may be a post-hoc rationalization rather than genuine reasoning — the model "knew" the answer and fabricated justification.

💸

Computational Cost

Extended reasoning chains consume significant compute at inference time. o1-style models may generate thousands of reasoning tokens for a single question, making them 10-100x more expensive than direct answering. The cost-accuracy tradeoff is steep.

📏

Evaluation Quality

Evaluating reasoning quality is fundamentally harder than evaluating final answers. Two different valid reasoning paths can both be correct. Process reward models themselves can have biases — rewarding verbose reasoning or familiar patterns over genuinely novel approaches.

⚡

Reasoning Shortcuts

Models sometimes learn superficial shortcuts that look like reasoning but aren't. Matching patterns in the problem structure rather than genuinely computing — performing well on benchmarks while failing on novel problem formats that require the same underlying logic.

📈

Scaling Inference Compute

While training compute scaling is well-studied (Chinchilla laws), optimal scaling of inference compute for reasoning is poorly understood. When should a model think longer? How do you decide when to stop reasoning? These questions lack principled answers.

🔀

Compositionality

Reasoning about novel combinations of known concepts remains fragile. Models can solve familiar problem types but struggle when multiple reasoning skills must be composed in new ways — the "compositional generalization" problem that has plagued AI for decades.

📄 Research

Key Papers & Resources

  • 📑
    Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models
    Wei et al., 2022 — The landmark paper that demonstrated how including reasoning steps in few-shot examples dramatically improves LLM performance on math, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning tasks.
  • 📑
    Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models
    Yao et al., 2023 — Generalized CoT from linear chains to tree-structured exploration, enabling systematic search through reasoning spaces with evaluation and backtracking.
  • 📑
    Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models
    Wang et al., 2023 — Introduced majority voting over multiple sampled reasoning paths, showing that diversity in reasoning approaches leads to more reliable final answers.
  • 📑
    Let's Verify Step by Step
    Lightman et al., 2023 — Demonstrated that process supervision (rewarding correct reasoning steps) dramatically outperforms outcome supervision (rewarding correct final answers) for mathematical reasoning.
  • 📑
    STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning With Reasoning
    Zelikman et al., 2022 — Showed that models can iteratively improve their own reasoning by generating rationales, filtering for correctness, and training on successful reasoning traces.
🔮 Future

Future Directions

  • Learned reasoning strategies — Moving beyond fixed prompting strategies toward models that learn when and how to reason. Adaptive systems that recognize problem difficulty and automatically select the appropriate reasoning depth — quick intuition for easy questions, deep deliberation for hard ones — without human-specified strategies.
  • Infinite reasoning time — Developing architectures where models can "think" for arbitrary amounts of time, scaling inference compute dynamically based on problem difficulty. This includes recurrent reasoning loops, iterative refinement, and reasoning time budgets that allocate compute where it's most needed.
  • Formal verification of chains — Connecting natural language reasoning with formal verification systems (proof checkers, SMT solvers, symbolic math engines) to mathematically guarantee the correctness of each reasoning step. Hybrid neuro-symbolic systems that combine the flexibility of LLMs with the rigor of formal methods.
  • Multimodal reasoning — Extending chain-of-thought to visual, spatial, and temporal reasoning. Models that can reason about geometric diagrams, physics simulations, chemical structures, and video sequences — thinking step-by-step through visual and physical problems, not just linguistic ones.
  • Reasoning distillation — Transferring the reasoning capabilities of large, expensive models (like o1) into smaller, faster models. Can a 7B model learn to reason like a 405B model by training on its reasoning traces? Early results are promising but achieving full transfer remains an open challenge.
