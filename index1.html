The four words · click each tab

Ideation, Idea, Concept, Product — these are not synonyms

People lose years because they use these four words interchangeably. Here's the brutal one-line version: an idea is a noun, a concept is an argument, a product is a habit.

⚙️

Ideation is a process, not a possession

You don't have ideation. You do ideation. It is the deliberate, repeatable, slightly uncomfortable activity of generating and then destroying large numbers of options.

Two halves that must never be mixed:

  • Divergence — go wide. Quantity over quality. Judgement is banned. Bad ideas are welcome because they unlock adjacent good ones.
  • Convergence — go narrow. Now judgement is mandatory. Cluster, compare, attack, kill.

The #1 mistake in every college brainstorm: someone says "that won't work" in minute two, and the room stops generating. Diverge and converge are different meetings.

🎚️

What "strategic" ideation adds

Random ideation gives you novelty. Strategic ideation gives you novelty that is aimed at something. You ideate inside constraints:

  • Who exactly are we serving? (not "students" — which students)
  • What outcome do they want that they can't get today?
  • Why us — what unfair advantage do we have? (campus access? a dataset? a skill?)
  • Why now — what changed in the world that makes this possible this year and not five years ago?
  • What must be true for this to work? (your assumptions, written down)
👆 Tap: the one-line test for strategic vs. random
If you can't finish the sentence "We are uniquely positioned to do this because ___" — you are not doing strategy, you are doing wishes.
💡

An IDEA is a sentence

It's a spark. It costs nothing, arrives unannounced in the shower or the bus, and cannot be proven wrong — which is exactly what makes it dangerous.

Example — carried through all four stages

💡 "An app that helps students find internships."

Sounds good in a group chat. Now notice what's missing:

  • Which students? 1st year or final year? Which branch? Which tier of college?
  • Find — is finding actually the hard part, or is it applying? Or getting shortlisted?
  • What do they do today instead? (LinkedIn? Seniors? A WhatsApp group? The placement cell?)
  • Why is today's method not good enough? How much does that hurt, in hours or rupees?
  • Why would anyone switch? Switching costs are the graveyard of good ideas.

None of those questions require code. All of them require leaving the building.

📐

A CONCEPT is a falsifiable argument

A concept is an idea that has been forced to make claims — claims specific enough that a stranger can look at it and say "no, that's wrong, and here's why."

A real concept always contains all six:

  1. WHO — a named, findable segment
  2. PROBLEM — what hurts, and how much (time, money, embarrassment)
  3. TODAY — the current workaround you must beat
  4. OUR MOVE — the specific intervention
  5. WHY BETTER — measurably, not adjectivally
  6. ASSUMPTIONS — what must be true, ranked by how badly you'd be wrecked if it's false

Cost: a few days. Deliverable: one page, a sketch, a landing page, a fake press release. Still zero lines of production code.

📝

Same example, now as a concept

WHO: 3rd-year CSE students at tier-2 and tier-3 colleges in Tamil Nadu.

PROBLEM: They don't lose internships by being unqualified — they lose them by finding out after the deadline. Average: 4 missed windows per semester.

TODAY: Random WhatsApp forwards from seniors + checking LinkedIn "when I remember."

OUR MOVE: Aggregate application deadlines from 200 companies; push a WhatsApp reminder 7 days before each one, filtered to their branch and CGPA band.

WHY BETTER: Deadline-driven, not discovery-driven. Delivered where they already look 80× a day instead of an inbox they check weekly.

MUST BE TRUE: (1) missed deadlines really are the dominant failure mode — riskiest; (2) deadline data is obtainable at low cost; (3) WhatsApp beats email for this.

Notice: this can now be attacked. A student can tell you in 30 seconds "no, my problem is I never get shortlisted." That 30 seconds just saved you 6 months.

📦

A PRODUCT is a habit that survives without you

Not a demo. Not a repo. Not a hackathon prototype. A product delivers value repeatedly, to people who are not your friends, while you are asleep.

TestRetention. Do they come back unprompted? Everything else is vanity
CostMonths to years — plus support, reliability, iteration, boredom
ProofSomeone gives up something scarce for it: money, time, or data

Fun trivia · the greatest solution-in-search-of-a-problem story ever told

🟨 It took 12 years to turn one idea into the Post-it Note

A scientist invented a glue that barely worked. That was the whole invention. Everything interesting happened in the twelve years afterwards.

1968 · THE IDEA
🧪

An accident, and a failure

At 3M, Dr. Spencer Silver is trying to make a super-strong aerospace adhesive. He produces the opposite: a glue that sticks lightly and peels off cleanly, reusably.

By every metric on his project brief, this is a failed experiment.

A solution with no problem attached. Technically fascinating, commercially useless.
1968–1974 · SIX YEARS OF SEARCHING
🔍

He refuses to let it die

Silver spends years running internal seminars across 3M, essentially asking colleagues: "I have this. What is it for?" Suggestions come and go: a sticky notice board, a spray. None stick (sorry).

Nobody could find the problem — because they kept starting from the glue.

Lesson: when you start from a solution, you can search for years and still miss.
1974 · THE CONCEPT ARRIVES
🎵

A different man, a real problem

Art Fry sings in his church choir. His paper bookmarks keep falling out of the hymnal, and he loses his place mid-hymn. A small, specific, weekly, genuinely annoying problem.

He remembers Silver's seminar. Problem, meet solution. Now — and only now — there's a concept: a bookmark that stays put and doesn't damage the page.

The invention was 6 years old. It became an idea worth building the day it met a problem.
1977 · THE FIRST LAUNCH FAILS
📉

"Press 'n Peel" flops

3M launches it in four cities. Sales are poor. Advertising described the product — and consumers had no idea what it was for. You cannot explain a Post-it in a print advert.

A launch failure is not proof the concept is wrong. It can be proof the distribution is wrong.

Same product. Wrong go-to-market. Nearly cancelled.
1979 · THE FIX — GIVE IT AWAY
🎁

The Boise blitz

Instead of advertising, 3M floods Boise, Idaho with free samples. Secretaries and managers use them once — and immediately understand.

Reorder rates come back extraordinarily high. The insight: this product has to be experienced, not described.

Some products can only be sold by being used. Sampling was the marketing.
1980 · PRODUCT
🏆

National launch. Then the world.

Post-it Notes go nationwide in 1980, international in 1981, and become one of the best-selling office products in history — sold in over 100 countries.

Idea 1968 → Product 1980. Twelve years.

The glue was never the product. The problem was the product.

The demoralising reading

"My odds are 1 in 3,000. Why bother?"

This is where most people stop listening. It's also the wrong reading — because the funnel is not a lottery.

🎯

The correct — and much more useful — reading

You don't beat the funnel by getting luckier. You beat it two ways:

  • Feed it more at the top — 3,000 ideas is a volume game, and volume is free.
  • Kill faster and cheaper on the way down — the funnel is only expensive if you discover the death after you've built. Move every death earlier.

That's the entire discipline. Not "have better ideas." Have more ideas, and kill them earlier.

3,000 ideas to one success. Meanwhile the average final-year team has exactly one idea, chosen in the first meeting, defended for eight months, and demoed at 2am the night before the review. We're not playing the odds — we're refusing to look at them.

Google was not the first search engine — it was roughly the twenty-first. Archie, Excite, Lycos, AltaVista, Yahoo, Infoseek, Ask Jeeves all came first. Facebook wasn't the first social network either — Friendster, MySpace and Orkut beat it. Being first is not the advantage. Being the one who understood the problem is.
🙋 Ask the room
  1. How many ideas did your project team seriously consider before picking one? Shout the number. (It will be 1, 2 or 3. Say nothing. Just let the funnel stay on screen.)
  2. What would change if the rule were: "you may not start building until you've written down 20 options and killed 19"?

Run the poll live. The funnel, the big numbers and the ✅ on the right answer stay locked until you tap — so take a show of hands per option first. The room will cluster around "10" and "100". The reveal at 3,000 gets an audible reaction — that's the point.

Source honesty: Stevens & Burley, Research-Technology Management (1997), "3,000 Raw Ideas = 1 Commercial Success." It's drawn from industrial R&D, so the exact ratio doesn't transfer perfectly to software — say so if asked. The shape is what matters. Same with the 95% figure: present it as "widely cited", commonly attributed to work associated with Clayton Christensen, not as a precisely measured constant.

The most important 20 seconds on this slide is the correct reading: more at the top, faster deaths on the way down. If they take one framework home, this is a strong candidate.

Ask #1 is a gentle trap and the room knows it. Do not mock the answer — land it with silence instead. Silence is more persuasive than a punchline here.

Act 2 · The most seductive trap in product

🪤 The trap of the "good idea"

Bad ideas are harmless — you abandon them by Tuesday. Good ideas are the dangerous ones, because a good idea gives you certainty without evidence — and certainty is what stops you asking questions.

🧠 Why a "good idea" is a trap, mechanically

It's a closed loop

A good idea contains its own justification. "An app that reminds you to drink water" — obviously useful, right? There's nothing left to investigate. The idea has answered its own question, so you stop asking.

It's socially rewarded

You describe it and people say "oh that's cool!" That reaction feels like validation. It is not. It is politeness. Nobody in the history of human conversation has replied "actually that's a terrible idea and here's my reasoning."

It's unfalsifiable

"People will love this" cannot be proven wrong until you've built it. So the only way to resolve your own excitement is to spend a year finding out. That's the trap closing.

🧲 The five biases that hold you inside the trap

🔍

1. Confirmation bias

Once you love the idea, you unconsciously hunt for evidence that agrees with you — and discount evidence that doesn't.

Its favourite disguise: asking "would you use this?" The answer is always yes. It costs the other person nothing to say yes. Never ask about the future. Ask about the past: "what did you do last time this happened?"

🪑

2. The IKEA effect

Researchers found people value furniture they assembled themselves far above identical pre-built furniture. Effort creates love, independent of quality.

Translation: the more code you write, the more you love your product — whether or not it's any good. Which is a strong argument for validating before you write the code.

🕳️

3. Sunk cost fallacy

"We've already put in six months, we can't stop now." But six months spent is gone either way — it can never be a reason to spend a seventh.

The only honest question is always forward-looking: "given what I know today, would I start this project today?" If no — stop today.

🔨

4. Curse of knowledge

You've just learned a powerful hammer — LLMs, blockchain, computer vision, RAG — and suddenly every problem looks like a nail.

Watch for the tell: your project title names a technology instead of an outcome. "Blockchain-based attendance system" is a solution wearing a problem's clothes. Who asked for the blockchain?

🏆

5. Survivorship bias

Every "just believe in your vision and ignore the doubters" story you've ever heard is told by a survivor.

The thousands who also ignored the doubters and were simply wrong don't get interviewed, don't write books, and don't give TED talks. You are learning from a filtered dataset.

🚨

Self-diagnosis: 6 warning signs

  • You describe your product before you describe the user
  • You have a logo and a name but no interviews
  • You say "people will love it", not "11 of 15 people told me..."
  • You feel defensive when questioned, not curious
  • Your pitch's first slide is a feature list
  • You say "there's no competition" — which means either no market, or you haven't looked
😂 Laugh line
"There's no competition in this space." Sir, there is always competition. If your app doesn't exist, people are solving it with WhatsApp, an Excel sheet, a phone call to their cousin, or simply suffering in silence. "Doing nothing" is your fiercest competitor — and it has a 100% market share, zero price and infinite brand loyalty.
🤯 Did you know
Kodak invented the first digital camera in 1975. The engineer, Steven Sasson, showed it to management — and was reportedly told it was cute, but "don't tell anyone about it." Kodak wasn't beaten by better technology. It was beaten by its own good idea — film — which was so profitable it became impossible to question. Kodak filed for bankruptcy in 2012.
🙋 Ask the room
  1. Honest hands: how many of those 6 warning signs apply to your current project? (Don't say the number out loud. Just know it.)
  2. Which bias do you think is hardest to escape? My answer: the IKEA effect — because the harder you work, the blinder you get. Do you agree?

The single most useful line on this slide: "Never ask about the future — ask about the past." Repeat it twice. "Would you use this?" is a fantasy question; "what did you do last time this happened?" is an archaeology question, and only archaeology gives you facts.

Kodak fact: attribute it carefully — Sasson has recounted the "that's cute, but don't tell anyone about it" reaction in interviews. Present it as his recollection, not a corporate minute. The lesson stands either way.

The "no competition" laugh line is a genuine teaching moment, not just a joke. Make sure you land "doing nothing has 100% market share." Students routinely benchmark against apps instead of against inertia — and inertia is what actually beats them.

Warning signs: ask them to count silently and privately. Public confession kills the honesty. You want internal discomfort, not embarrassment.

If you remember one slide, remember this one

Solution First vs Problem First

Same team. Same talent. Same technology. Same amount of work. Only the order of the steps is different — and the order decides the outcome.

❌ Solution first · "I'll build it and they will come"

🔨 The exciting road to nowhere

  1. "I have a cool idea!" or "I just learned this amazing technology"
  2. Start building immediately. This is the fun part. Weeks disappear happily
  3. Product works. It's genuinely impressive. Screenshots look great
  4. Now go looking for who needs it
  5. Discover that nobody in particular does. Panic quietly
  6. Conclude that the problem is awareness → add marketing
  7. Still nothing → conclude the product is missing features → add features
  8. Team gets tired. Exams arrive. It dies without a funeral
Emotional profile: thrilling for 3 months, then quietly humiliating for 9.
Money spent before learning anything: all of it.
VS
✅ Problem first · "Fall in love with the problem"

🔍 The boring road that works

  1. Find one specific person with a recurring, expensive pain
  2. Quantify it: how often? how many hours? how many rupees? how much embarrassment?
  3. Study today's workaround — the Excel sheet, the WhatsApp group, the peon. That is your real competitor
  4. Write the concept: who, problem, today, our move, why better, assumptions
  5. Rank assumptions by "how dead am I if this is false?" Test the riskiest one with the cheapest possible thing — a conversation, a form, a poster, a fake button
  6. Only now build — the smallest thing that removes the pain
  7. Measure whether they come back. If they don't, you learned it in weeks, not years
  8. Earn the right to build more
Emotional profile: tedious and awkward for 3 weeks, then increasingly exciting forever.
Money spent before learning: almost none.
Solution-first is fun, then painful.
Problem-first is painful, then fun.
You don't get to skip the pain. You only get to choose where in the timeline it happens — and how much it costs when it arrives.
⚖️

Important honesty: solution-first isn't always wrong

Some of humanity's biggest leaps were solution-first. The transistor, the laser, GPUs, and large language models all existed before anyone knew the killer application. Research and deep tech legitimately start from capability.

But note the rule: a technology push only becomes a product when it finally meets a problem. Post-it took 12 years to find its hymnal. The lab can afford that wait. Your final-year project cannot.

💬

The sentence that fixes 80% of student projects

Instead of: "I'm building an X that does Y."
Say: "___ struggles to ___, and today they cope by ___, which costs them ___."

If you cannot fill in all four blanks with things a real named human actually told you, you are still solution-first — no matter how good your architecture is.

👆 Tap: the four blanks, filled in properly
"Final-year students in placement season struggle to track 40 application deadlines across 12 portals, and today they cope by a shared Google Sheet nobody updates, which costs them roughly 4 missed opportunities per semester."

Now the solution is almost boring to design — and that's exactly the point.
😂 Laugh line
Solution-first sounds like: "We've built an AI-powered blockchain IoT platform for sustainable smart campuses." Problem-first sounds like: "The lab printer is always broken and nobody knows which one works." Guess which one gets a standing ovation at the review — and guess which one students would actually use every single day.
🤯 Did you know
Uri Levine, co-founder of Waze (sold to Google for over $1 billion), titled his book "Fall in Love with the Problem, Not the Solution." His reasoning is brutally practical: your solution will change many times. If you love the solution, every change feels like a defeat. If you love the problem, every change feels like progress.
🙋 Ask the room
  1. Which road is your project on right now? Left or right? Point with your hand. (Most will point left. That's fine — it's Friday, not a funeral. It's fixable.)
  2. Fill in the four blanks for your project, out loud. Volunteer? 2 points to your team if you can do it without using the word "app."

This is THE slide. Spend 4–5 minutes here, more than any other teaching slide. Walk down the left column pointing at each step and let the room recognise themselves. There will be uncomfortable laughter at steps 6 and 7 ("add marketing", "add features") — that laughter is the learning.

Then read the quote slowly. "You don't get to skip the pain, you only get to choose where in the timeline it happens." Pause. This reframes the whole seminar from "be smarter" to "reorder your suffering", which is a much more actionable idea.

Do not skip the honesty card. If you claim solution-first is always wrong, the sharpest students in the room will correctly think of counter-examples and mentally check out. Acknowledging deep tech and research earns you the right to insist on problem-first for their projects.

Ask #2 is the highest-value 90 seconds in Act 2. Get two or three volunteers to fill in the four blanks live. Coach them in real time: when they say "an app for", stop them and make them restart. Banning the word "app" forces problem language.

Autopsy 1 of 3 · Solution-first · $120 million

🧃 Juicero: the most beautifully engineered answer to nothing

The clearest, funniest and most expensive solution-first failure of the last decade. Every detail of it was excellent — except the one that mattered.

🧃
Juicero
Founded 2013 · Launched 2016 · Dead September 2017
0 raised — from some of the best-known investors in Silicon Valley,
including Google's venture arm and Kleiner Perkins
The product A $699 Wi-Fi-connected cold-press juicer (later cut to $399), plus proprietary single-serve "Produce Packs" at roughly $5–8 each, available only by subscription.
The engineering Genuinely extraordinary. The press was reported to exert around four tons of force — the company boasted it could lift two cars. Custom aluminium castings. A scanner that read a QR code on each pack to verify freshness and refuse expired produce.
The pitch Fresh, cold-pressed, zero-mess, perfectly portioned juice at home. The founder openly invoked Apple-style design obsession.
The collapse April 2017: journalists at Bloomberg published something devastating — they squeezed the Produce Packs with their bare hands and got the same juice, roughly as fast. The video went everywhere. Sales were suspended within months.
☠️ Cause of death: the $699 machine was not required to obtain the outcome. The pack was the product. The press was decoration.

🔬 Problem-first autopsy — where exactly did it break?

Real job "I want fresh, healthy juice without effort and without mess." This is a completely legitimate job. The problem was real.
Who solved it The pack already solved ~95% of that job. Pre-washed, pre-cut, pre-portioned produce in a sealed bag is the innovation.
The error They fell in love with the hardware — the part that was fun to engineer, patentable, demo-able and investable — and never tested whether it was necessary.
Untested assumption "Customers need a machine to extract juice from our pack." Nobody ever ran the experiment that would have falsified it. Cost of that experiment: ₹0 and 30 seconds. Two hands.

🎯 The $120 million question nobody asked

"What happens if we just squeeze the bag?"

Somebody in that building almost certainly thought it. Junior. In a room full of confident senior people. Next to a founder comparing himself to Steve Jobs. And said nothing.

The transferable lesson: if you can remove your product and the user still gets the outcome — you don't have a product. You have an accessory.
😂 Laugh line
The machine had four tons of pressing force and Wi-Fi. Four tons. For juice. It could lift two cars, and it could be defeated by a firm handshake. Somewhere out there is an engineer whose CV honestly says "designed the DRM system for a bag of fruit."
🤯 Did you know
Juicero's packs would refuse to be pressed after their expiry date — the machine scanned a QR code and declined. So people who paid ₹50,000 for a juicer discovered that their fruit had an operating system. Every "smart" feature you add is a new way for your product to say no to a paying customer.
🙋 Ask the room
  1. Apply the test to your own project: if you deleted your app tomorrow, could users still get the outcome another way? How much worse would their life be — honestly, in minutes or rupees?
  2. If you were the intern who spotted it, how would you actually have raised it without ending your career? (Real answer: don't argue — just do the experiment and show the video. Evidence beats opinion in every organisation on earth.)

This story earns the biggest laugh of the seminar. Use it. Build it in order: the funding, the world-class investors, the four tons of force, the QR-code freshness verification — get the room genuinely impressed — then deliver the hands. The bigger the build-up, the harder the fall lands.

Then immediately turn serious. "Everybody laughs at Juicero. But be honest — how many of you have built something where the elegant part was the part nobody needed?" That turn from laughter to self-recognition is the whole purpose of the slide.

Ask #2 is quietly the most valuable career advice in the deck. Students will one day be the junior person in a room full of confident seniors. Teach them the move: don't argue, run the cheapest possible experiment and bring the evidence. Say explicitly: "Evidence is how junior people win arguments with senior people. It is the only reliable way."

Accuracy note: Bloomberg's April 2017 report is the well-documented trigger; the "four tons"/"lift two cars" claims come from the company's own marketing. Say "reported" and "the company boasted" — the caution is itself a teaching moment.

The other side · problem first · what it actually looks like

Two companies that started with a problem they personally had

Notice what's missing from both origin stories: no market research report, no big idea, no technology. Just a specific person, genuinely annoyed, about something specific.

🛏️
Airbnb
2007 · San Francisco · started with an air mattress
Their problem Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia could not afford their rent. Not a market opportunity. Not a trend report. A bill they could not pay.
Someone else's A big international design conference was coming to San Francisco and the city's hotels were sold out. Designers were flying in with nowhere to sleep.
The test They put three airbeds on their floor, built a simple page in a weekend, and charged $80 a night. Three guests came. No app. No platform. No funding. A weekend and an air mattress.
The hard years Investors rejected them repeatedly — strangers sleeping in strangers' homes sounded absurd. To survive in 2008 they sold novelty election-themed cereal boxes and reportedly made around $30,000. Founders do unglamorous things.
The insight Bookings in New York were weak. Instead of writing an algorithm, they flew to New York, knocked on hosts' doors, and photographed the apartments themselves with a borrowed camera. Bookings jumped. The listings weren't bad — the photographs were.
✅ The lesson: the fix was not in the code. It was in the living rooms. Paul Graham's advice to them became famous: "Do things that don't scale."
📦
Dropbox
2007 · began with a forgotten USB drive
His problem Drew Houston kept forgetting his USB drive. Repeatedly. Annoyingly. He started writing code for himself on a bus journey — not to found a company, but to stop being irritated.
The insight Sync tools already existed and were terrible — confusing, unreliable, technical. His insight was about invisibility: it shouldn't feel like a product at all. It should just be a folder that works.
The problem with proving it File sync is brutally hard to build and impossible to explain in words. Building the full product first would have cost a year — before learning whether anyone cared.
The cheapest possible test So he made a three-minute demo video instead — narrated, deliberately packed with in-jokes for the tech community it targeted — and posted it online. The beta waiting list reportedly jumped from around 5,000 to 75,000 people overnight.
The punchline The product barely existed yet. He had validated demand for a product he hadn't finished — with a video.
✅ The lesson: the cheapest MVP is often not software at all. A video, a landing page, a poster, a WhatsApp broadcast, a spreadsheet you run by hand.
🎯

Pattern 1 · Start with a problem you have

You get a free, instant, unlimited-access expert user: yourself. You can smell a fake solution immediately. Both founders were their own first customer.

Caution: it's a great start, not proof. You must still check that others share the problem — you might be weird.

🪜

Pattern 2 · The test is smaller than the product

Airbnb tested with three airbeds. Dropbox tested with a video. Neither built the real thing to find out if the real thing was wanted.

Ask yourself: what's the smallest, most embarrassing version of this that still tests my riskiest assumption?

🚪

Pattern 3 · The answer is outside the building

Airbnb's growth unlock was a camera in someone's apartment, not a feature. You will not find your insight in your IDE, your dorm room, or ChatGPT.

Steve Blank's rule for startups: "Get out of the building." The single highest-return activity available to you costs nothing but courage.

😂 Laugh line
Airbnb is now worth tens of billions of dollars, and it began with two broke designers thinking "what if strangers slept on our floor for cash?" If you had pitched that at a final-year project review, you would have been sent to the HOD. Possibly to the police.
🤯 Did you know
Netflix's founders had to answer one terrifying question before starting: would a disc survive the post? They didn't model it, commission a study, or build a logistics platform. They put a disc in a cheap envelope, posted it to themselves, and waited to see if it arrived intact. Total cost of validating a billion-dollar business model: the price of a stamp.
🙋 Ask the room
  1. What is your forgotten-USB-drive? A thing that annoys you repeatedly, that you've stopped noticing because you've normalised it?
  2. What's the stamp-sized experiment for your project — the one that costs under ₹500 and under 2 hours, but would genuinely change your mind if it failed?

The strongest detail in the Airbnb story is the camera, not the airbeds. Everyone has heard the airbed origin. Almost nobody knows the growth unlock was flying to New York and physically photographing apartments. Emphasise it: an engineer's instinct is to fix conversion with code; the actual fix was a camera and a doorbell.

The strongest detail in Dropbox is the sequencing: video first, product later. Say clearly, "he sold something that didn't exist — not fraudulently, but as an experiment. That's the difference between lying and testing: he was willing to be told no."

Accuracy: the Dropbox waitlist numbers (~5,000 → ~75,000 overnight) are Houston's own widely-repeated account. The cereal-box figure (~$30,000) is the commonly cited number from Airbnb's founders. Say "reportedly" and move on — the magnitude is the point.

Ask #2 is the setup for Act 3. Collect two or three "stamp-sized experiments" from the room; you'll reference them on the MVP ladder slide (26).

Closer to home · four Indian insights, not four Indian ideas

🇮🇳 The best Indian products won on insight, not novelty

Not one of these had an original idea. Restaurants, shops, brokers and phone networks all existed. Each one won by understanding one specific thing about Indian users that outsiders got wrong.

🍽️
Zomato
2008 · began as "Foodiebay", inside an office
The observation Deepinder Goyal, working at Bain & Company in Delhi, noticed colleagues physically queueing up to look at the office's paper menu cards at lunchtime. He didn't run a survey. He watched a queue.
The test He scanned the menus and put them on the company intranet. That's it. Traffic exploded internally — proof, measured in days, using a scanner.
Why it worked He tested observed behaviour, not stated intent. Nobody had said "I want a food website." They had, however, formed a queue — and a queue is data.
✅ Watch what people do, especially where they wait, queue or complain.
📦
Flipkart · Cash on Delivery
Founded 2007 · COD introduced 2010
The lazy diagnosis "Indians don't shop online because they're not tech-savvy." Comfortable, condescending, and wrong.
The real insight Indians didn't distrust the internet. They distrusted paying money before receiving the goods — a completely rational position in a market with weak recourse and very low card penetration at the time.
The move Cash on Delivery. Not a technology. Not an algorithm. A trust mechanism — pay the human standing in front of you, after you've seen the box.
✅ It became the industry standard and helped unlock Indian e-commerce for hundreds of millions of people. Sometimes the product feature is a payment term.
📊
Zerodha
2010 · bootstrapped · no outside funding · no advertising
The insight Every broker charged a percentage of trade value — which meant the more actively you traded, the more you were silently punished. Nithin Kamath's move: a flat fee per trade regardless of size.
The second insight Most Indians weren't avoiding investing because of fees — they were avoiding it because they didn't understand it. So Zerodha built Varsity, a genuinely free education platform. They solved the problem before the transaction.
✅ Grew to become India's largest retail broker — profitable, bootstrapped, and famously ad-free. Same "idea" as every other broker. Completely different insight.
📱
Jio + UPI
Both landed in 2016 · India's biggest behaviour change
The problem Mobile data was priced as a luxury — hundreds of rupees per GB — so people rationed it. And moving money required cash, or knowing account numbers and IFSC codes.
The moves Jio collapsed the price of data by an order of magnitude, and India went on to become one of the world's largest consumers of mobile data. UPI (built by NPCI) made payments free, instant, interoperable and tied to a simple ID — growing from near-zero in 2016 to well over 10 billion transactions a month.
✅ Neither invented a new technology. Both removed friction and price from something people already wanted to do. Sometimes the highest-impact product change is a subtraction.

🧩 The common thread — worth writing down

🔎 Zomato watched a queue, not a survey.

🤝 Flipkart fixed trust, not technology.

💸 Zerodha fixed a pricing model, not a product.

🧹 Jio & UPI removed friction, and invented nothing.

None of these are technology breakthroughs. All of them are problem breakthroughs.

😂 Laugh line
Zomato began because a man got tired of standing in a queue to read a menu card. Meanwhile we're all sitting here trying to invent "an AI-powered blockchain metaverse for sustainable agriculture." The billion-dollar company started with a laminated sheet of paper and mild irritation.
🤯 Did you know
Cash on Delivery is a genuinely Indian product innovation that most Western e-commerce playbooks never needed and never predicted. Your local context is not a limitation — it's proprietary information that Silicon Valley cannot buy, download or fake. You know things they don't.
🙋 Ask the room
  1. Where's the queue? Name a place on this campus where people physically wait, repeat themselves, or fill the same form twice. (Collect 3–4 answers — we'll use one in Act 4.)
  2. Name one thing that is true about Indian users that a product built in California would get completely wrong.

This slide is where an Indian student audience leans in. The global examples are inspiring but feel distant; these feel available. Emphasise repeatedly that none of these were new ideas — restaurants, shops, brokers and telecom all existed. They won on insight.

Zomato's queue is the star. "He watched a queue. A queue is data." Say it twice — it gives students a concrete, zero-cost research technique they can use on this campus this week.

Proof that the problem — not the technology — is the variable

🔄 The Pivot Hall of Fame: same tech, new problem, different destiny

These companies didn't get better engineers or better funding. They kept the solution and swapped the problem. That single move is the difference between the graveyard and the hall of fame.

👓

Google Glass → Glass Enterprise

Failed Consumers: a $1,500 answer to no question, and a privacy problem for everyone nearby.

Worked Factory and warehouse workers whose hands are full and who need assembly instructions in their line of sight instead of on a clipboard across the room.

Companies including Boeing and AGCO reported real productivity and error-rate gains. Identical hardware. The problem changed, and suddenly it was valuable.

📸

Burbn → Instagram

Failed Burbn was a location check-in app with too many features: check-ins, plans, points, photos. Confusing to explain, impossible to love.

Worked The data showed people ignored everything except photos. Systrom and Krieger deleted almost the entire product and kept three things: photo, filter, share.

Reportedly 25,000 users on day one. Acquired for $1 billion within about 18 months, with a team of roughly 13 people. The winning move was deletion.

💬

Glitch → Slack

Failed Stewart Butterfield's team spent years building an online game called Glitch. It shut down.

Worked To build the game, they'd made an internal chat tool for themselves. The game died. The tool became Slack — one of the fastest-growing business products in history.

Butterfield did this twice. An earlier failed game, Game Neverending, produced Flickr. Twice, the by-product was worth more than the product.

🐦

Odeo → Twitter

Failed Odeo was a podcasting platform. Then Apple built podcasts directly into iTunes — and a startup cannot out-distribute a default.

Worked During a hack week, a side project for posting short status updates by SMS emerged. That side project was Twitter.

Lesson: when the world moves against you, the assets you keep are your team, your code and your learning — not your idea.

🛒

Grofers → Blinkit

Struggled As Grofers, scheduled grocery delivery was a nice-to-have. "Order today, arrives in a 4-hour window tomorrow" competes with a shop 200 metres away.

Worked Reframed to 10-minute delivery. That changes the job entirely: no longer weekly planning, but "I've run out of something right now and guests are arriving."

Same warehouses, same app, same category. Different job-to-be-done — and therefore a different business.

🎓

What this means for you

A pivot is not a failure. A pivot is what problem-first looks like when the first problem turned out to be wrong.

  • Your skills transfer. Your code often transfers. Your attachment to the original idea is the only thing that must die.
  • So build things that make pivoting cheap: small scope, modular code, short feedback loops, no ego in the name.

Bonus trivia: YouTube launched as a video dating site. PayPal began by beaming money between Palm Pilots. Nintendo started out selling playing cards in 1889.

😂 Laugh line
Stewart Butterfield tried to build a video game twice and failed twice — accidentally producing Flickr and then Slack along the way. At this point somebody should just fund him to make another game purely to see what falls out of it.
🤯 Did you know
Google Glass never actually died. It quietly became Glass Enterprise Edition and shipped into factories and warehouses for years. The technology was never the problem. The user was. Before you conclude your project is a failure, ask whether you've simply pointed it at the wrong person.
🙋 Ask the room
  1. Think of an abandoned project of yours from the ice breaker. Is there a different user for whom it would have been valuable? (This question has restarted more dead projects than any other.)
  2. Instagram won by deleting features. What would you delete from your project if you were only allowed to keep three things?

This is the hope slide. Act 2 has been relentlessly about failure; students who recognised themselves on slide 12 need a way out. Put this one in explicitly: "everything you've built is reusable. Only your attachment has to die."

If you're behind schedule, this is the safest slide to cut — but if you cut it, you must move the "a pivot is not a failure" line into slide 19 or 30, because the room needs it.

Instagram's deletion story is the most actionable one for final-year projects, which are almost universally over-scoped. Ask #2 gets them to scope down without you having to lecture them about scope.

Tool 2 of 6 · from the Toyota Production System

🕳️ The 5 Whys: the problem you see is never the problem you have

Ask "why?" five times. It sounds childish. It is the most reliable way to move from a symptom you'd waste a year on to a root cause you can fix this week.

🏛️ A classic case: a crumbling monument

Symptom: the stonework of a famous monument is deteriorating badly. The obvious solution: budget crores for stone restoration, forever. Click the button above (or any row) to drill down.

WHY 1 Why is the stone deteriorating?
Because it's cleaned very frequently with harsh chemical detergents.
WHY 2 Why is it cleaned so frequently?
Because of an enormous quantity of bird droppings.
WHY 3 Why are there so many birds?
Because they come to feed on the fat spiders living on the monument.
WHY 4 Why are there so many spiders?
Because they feed on swarms of midges that gather there at dusk.
WHY 5 Why do the midges swarm there at dusk?
Because the monument's floodlights are switched on right at dusk — and the light attracts them.
✅ ROOT The fix: switch the floodlights on one hour later.
The midges — and therefore the spiders, the birds, the droppings, the chemicals and the stone damage — collapse. Cost of the fix: changing a timer. Versus crores of rupees of stonework, forever.

Honesty note: this is a much-loved teaching case whose finer details are probably embellished over the decades. I'm telling you that on purpose — questioning the provenance of a good story is exactly the habit this seminar is about. The technique it teaches is entirely real.

🎓 The same drill, on this campus

Symptom: "Students don't use the official placement portal."

  • Why? They hear about openings somewhere else first.
  • Why? The portal never notifies them — you must remember to log in and check.
  • Why don't they log in? They've forgotten the password, and a reset means emailing an administrator and waiting two days.
  • Why is reset manual? There's no self-service reset — it was built once as a project and handed over.
  • Why was it built that way? Because it was scoped as a record-keeping system for the placement cell, not a notification system for students.
✅ Root cause: the product was designed for the administrator, not the user.
Wrong fix: "run a campaign telling students to use the portal."
Right fix: push notifications where students already are + self-service login.

⚠️ Three ways people get the 5 Whys wrong

  • Blaming people instead of systems. If your chain ends in "because students are lazy" or "because the staff don't care", you've stopped early. Ask why the system makes that behaviour rational. Toyota's version always drives towards a process, never a person.
  • Stopping at the first plausible answer. Answer 1 always sounds sufficient. That's precisely why the technique demands five.
  • Treating "5" as sacred. Sometimes it's three, sometimes seven. Stop when you reach something you can actually change.
😂 Laugh line
The 5 Whys is just being a four-year-old with a project budget. Every toddler on earth already has this skill and we spend eighteen years systematically training it out of them — then hire expensive consultants to bring it back.
🤯 Did you know
The 5 Whys came from Sakichi Toyoda and became a pillar of the Toyota Production System — the manufacturing philosophy that Lean Startup was later built on. The methodology you'll use to build software was invented on a car factory floor by people asking annoying questions.
🙋 Ask the room
  1. Give me a campus complaint — anything. Wi-Fi, queues, timetables, buses. Let's run the 5 Whys live, right now, as a room. (Do it out loud. It takes 90 seconds and it will be the moment they remember.)
  2. Where did our chain end — at a person or at a system? If it ended at a person, we stopped too early. Keep going.

Reveal the monument chain one row at a time using the button — do not dump it. Let the room guess the next "why". Somebody will get the spiders and be delighted with themselves. Give them points.

Then do Ask #1 live. This is non-negotiable — it's the difference between them understanding the tool and being able to use it. Take a real campus complaint from the room and drive it down five levels on the whiteboard. Watch for the moment the room realises the fix is much smaller and cheaper than the one they'd assumed.

The "blame systems, not people" rule is the most important guardrail. Student chains almost always terminate at "because students are lazy" or "because the admin is slow." Stop them and push: why is that behaviour rational given the system? That single reframe is a genuine professional skill.

Tool 3 of 6 · qualify the pain before you spend a year on it

💊 Painkiller, vitamin, or candy?

A real problem is not the same as a problem worth solving. Before you build anything, classify the pain honestly — because each type demands a completely different strategy, and only one is forgiving.

💊

Painkiller

Best odds. Build these.

Solves an acute, urgent, recurring pain. The user is already actively looking for a fix and often already paying — in money, or in hours.

  • They can name the last time it happened, and how it felt
  • They have a workaround — a hack, a sheet, a person they bother
  • Selling is easy; you're interrupting a search, not creating a want
✅ "I missed an application deadline and lost the internship."
✅ "Attendance reconciliation takes our staff 6 hours every week."
🍊

Vitamin

Hard. Possible. Slow.

Genuinely good for them, but there's no urgency. Nobody wakes up at 2am because they haven't been taking their vitamins.

  • Users agree it's valuable — and still don't act
  • Signing up is easy. Retention is brutal. Week 3 is where they vanish
  • Wins only with habit design, streaks, social pressure or someone else paying
⚠️ "An app that reminds you to drink water."
⚠️ "Learn a new language 10 minutes a day."
Not impossible — Duolingo is a vitamin, and it's enormous. But look at how much game design that required.
🍬

Candy

Fun. Spiky. Fragile.

Pure delight, zero utility. Can spread explosively — and can vanish just as fast, because novelty is a spike, not a business.

  • Growth can be spectacular and cheap
  • But there's no reason to return once the novelty is spent
  • Monetising is awkward — remember purple ketchup and Vine
🎲 Great for a hackathon or a portfolio piece. Dangerous as a two-year bet unless you have a plan for what happens after the novelty dies.
🔥

Five questions that classify any pain in 2 minutes

  • 1. Frequency — how often does it happen? Daily beats weekly beats once-a-semester. Rare pain is almost never a business.
  • 2. Intensity — what does it cost when it happens? In rupees, hours, marks, or embarrassment. Get a number.
  • 3. Existing spend — are they already paying something to cope, in money or time? An existing budget is the single strongest signal there is.
  • 4. Search behaviour — have they actively looked for a solution? Someone who has already googled it is a customer. Someone who hasn't is an audience.
  • 5. The hair-on-fire test — if a person's hair is on fire, they'll buy a bucket of water at any price and won't ask about your features, your UI or your tech stack. Is your customer's hair on fire?
🗣️

How to ask — The Mom Test by Rob Fitzpatrick

Named for a simple idea: ask questions so good that even your mother couldn't lie to you about your idea. Three rules:

  • Talk about their life, not your idea. The moment you describe your idea, they start being polite — and politeness destroys data.
  • Ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future. "What did you do last time?" ✅   "Would you use this?" ❌
  • Talk less. Listen more. If you're talking more than a third of the time, it's a pitch, not an interview.

❌ Bad data

Compliments ("cool idea!"), fluff ("I usually…", "I always…"), and their feature suggestions.

✅ Good data

Facts about the past, specific numbers, and commitments — time, money, reputation, an introduction.

😂 Laugh line
Every college produces roughly forty water-reminder apps per year. Nobody has ever been dehydrated because of insufficient notifications. They were dehydrated because getting up is effort — and your push notification is not a bottle.
🤯 Did you know
The strongest validation signal isn't a survey, a like, or a sign-up. It's a commitment of something scarce: money, calendar time, reputation, or an introduction to their boss. "I'd definitely use this" costs nothing and means nothing. "Here's ₹100, tell me when it's ready" is data.
🙋 Ask the room
  1. Painkiller, vitamin or candy? Classify your own project out loud. (Be gentle — a lot of hands will honestly say vitamin. That's a useful discovery, not a verdict.)
  2. Name a painkiller problem on this campus where somebody is already spending money or hours to cope with it. 3 points — because that's the hardest and most valuable question of the day.

Be fair to vitamins. Duolingo, Headspace and every fitness app are vitamins and some are huge — but they win through enormous investment in habit mechanics. Say plainly: "a vitamin is a valid business and a terrible first project, because it requires the hardest skill in product — habit formation — and you have eight months."

The hair-on-fire test is the most memorable framing and it comes from venture-capital folklore. Use it as the sanity check they'll remember in the shower.

The Mom Test is the single most practical book recommendation in this seminar. It's short and cheap. Say the title clearly, twice, and tell them it's a weekend read. Of everything today, this is the highest-leverage follow-up.

Ask #1 will produce a lot of honest "vitamin" answers. Do not let the room deflate. Respond: "good — now you know what you're up against, and you can either design for habit or find the painkiller version of the same problem."

Tool 4 of 6 · and a research finding that will annoy you

🔀 Diverge, then converge — and why group brainstorming is a myth

Ideation has two modes that must never happen in the same room at the same time. Mixing them is the reason every college brainstorm produces the same three ideas.

💠 The Double Diamond — British Design Council, 2005

DIAMOND 1 · PROBLEM SPACE

1. Discover — diverge

Go wide on the problem. Interviews, observation, queues, complaints, data. Do not propose solutions yet. Collect pain.

DIAMOND 1 · PROBLEM SPACE

2. Define — converge

Narrow to one sharp problem statement you're willing to bet on. Output: a single sentence, and a How Might We question.

DIAMOND 2 · SOLUTION SPACE

3. Develop — diverge

Now go wide on solutions. Many options, deliberately including bad ones. Sketch, don't build.

DIAMOND 2 · SOLUTION SPACE

4. Deliver — converge

Pick one, test the riskiest assumption, build the smallest version, measure, repeat.

Where nearly every student project goes wrong: it starts at step 3. The entire first diamond — the problem space — gets skipped because it's the part that isn't fun and can't be shown to anyone.

🗣️

The uncomfortable research: group brainstorming underperforms

Since a landmark 1958 Yale study (Taylor, Berry & Block), study after study has found the same thing: the same number of people, working alone and then pooling their ideas, produce more ideas and better ideas than a group brainstorming together.

Four well-documented reasons:

  • Production blocking — only one person can speak at a time, so everyone else is waiting instead of thinking.
  • Evaluation apprehension — the fear of sounding stupid in front of peers, seniors or a professor. This silences exactly the unusual ideas you need.
  • Social loafing — in a group, individual effort quietly drops. Someone else will carry it.
  • Anchoring & conformity — the first idea spoken aloud sets the direction, and the rest of the session becomes variations on it.
☠️ Which is exactly why your project group meeting produced three ideas, all of them versions of whatever the loudest person said first.
✍️

The fix: brainwriting — silent first, loud second

  1. Frame the question as a single How Might We on the board. Everyone reads it.
  2. Silence. 6 minutes. Every person writes as many ideas as possible, alone. No talking, no laptops, no phones. Target 10 each.
  3. Round-robin share. One idea each, going round, no criticism allowed — just capture. Everyone contributes before anyone debates.
  4. Build on each other's. Second silent round: 3 minutes, now writing ideas inspired by what you heard. This is where the good stuff appears.
  5. Cluster into themes on the wall.
  6. Vote privately — everyone gets 3 dots, placed simultaneously. Private voting means the senior person's opinion can't anchor everyone else's.
✅ Six people × 10 ideas = 60 options in six minutes, and the shy student's idea survives contact with the room.
😂 Laugh line
Corporate brainstorming: eight people, one whiteboard, two hours, free samosas. Output: the idea the most senior person mentioned in minute three, rewritten in nicer handwriting. The samosas were the only reliable deliverable.
🤯 Did you know
The "no bad ideas" rule was popularised by advertising executive Alex Osborn in the 1950s — and it's genuinely useful, but only during divergence. If you keep it switched on during convergence, you can never kill anything — and a team that can't kill ideas will slowly build all of them badly.
🙋 Ask the room
  1. In your last group project meeting, who decided the idea? Was it the best idea, or the most confidently delivered one? (Be honest.)
  2. How many of you have had an idea in a meeting and not said it because you thought it sounded stupid? Hands up. (Most of the room. Then say: "that's evaluation apprehension, it has a name, and brainwriting is the cure.")

This slide gives them a genuinely new and immediately usable process. The "group brainstorming is worse than working alone" finding is counterintuitive enough that the room will push back — good. The four mechanisms explain why, so lean on those rather than just asserting the result.

Ask #2 is the emotional hook. Almost every hand goes up. Naming it ("evaluation apprehension") gives students permission to stop blaming themselves, and brainwriting gives them a fix they can run in their next team meeting without anyone's approval.

If you have a spare 60 seconds, demonstrate step 2 live: put a How Might We on the screen, say "silence, 60 seconds, write as many as you can, alone" and actually do it. The room will be startled by how many ideas they produce. Then ask how many wrote more than five.

The "skipped first diamond" point is the connective tissue of the whole seminar — it's the structural restatement of solution-first vs problem-first. Say it explicitly: "the first diamond is the problem space, and skipping it is solution-first thinking."

Concept → evidence · climb from the bottom, never skip a rung

🪜 The MVP ladder: buy evidence as cheaply as possible

Every rung buys you information. The rungs get more expensive as you climb. The discipline is simple and almost nobody follows it: never climb a rung until the one below it passes.

🪜 Rung 1 · Conversation ₹0 · 1 day

10 interviews using The Mom Test. Ask about the past, never the future. Tests: does this problem even exist, and does it hurt? If you skip this rung, every rung above it is guesswork with a nicer UI.

🪜 Rung 2 · Poster / message ₹0–500 · 1 day

A physical poster on the notice board, or one WhatsApp broadcast. Tests: does anyone react when this is described in public? Silence is a result. A very cheap, very useful result.

🪜 Rung 3 · Fake door / landing page ₹0 · 1–2 days

A one-page site with a real "Sign up" button that leads to "Thanks — we'll notify you at launch." Tests: will they take an action, not just express an opinion? Buffer famously validated demand — and pricing — this way before building.

🪜 Rung 4 · Explainer video ₹0 · 2–3 days

Show the product working — even if it doesn't exist yet. This is exactly what Dropbox did, and it took the waitlist from ~5,000 to ~75,000 people before the product was finished.

🪜 Rung 5 · Concierge MVP ₹0 · 1–2 weeks

Do the whole service manually, by hand, for 5 users. They know it's manual. You are the algorithm. Tests: does solving it actually make their life better? This is where you discover the problem's real shape — the part no interview reveals.

🪜 Rung 6 · Wizard of Oz ₹0 · 1–2 weeks

Looks fully automated from the front. It's you behind the curtain. Zappos began with founder Nick Swinmurn photographing shoes in local shops and buying them at retail whenever someone ordered — no warehouse, no inventory. Amazon later acquired Zappos for around $1.2 billion.

🪜 Rung 7 · Single-feature product weeks

The smallest real, working thing that completely solves one job for one segment. Tests: retention. Do they come back without being reminded?

🪜 Rung 8 · The real product months+

Only now. And notice: by this point you're not guessing anymore — you're executing against evidence you personally collected. This is what "de-risked" actually means.

💰

The strongest signal of all: pre-selling

Above every rung sits one thing: somebody gives you money, or a signature, before the product exists.

"I'd definitely use it" is free to say. ₹100 now, or a signed letter from the placement cell, is a fact. Take a deposit. Take a commitment. Take a calendar slot.

⚖️

The rule people break

Define pass/fail before you run the test.

Write it down: "If fewer than 6 of 15 students tell me they missed a deadline last semester, we stop and pick a different problem."

Without a number written in advance, every result will look like encouragement — because you'll interpret it after you already know it.

🤔

"Isn't the fake door dishonest?"

Good question — and the line is real. It's a legitimate test if you don't take money for something you can't deliver, you're honest that it's coming soon, and you actually follow up.

It becomes wrong the moment you'd be unwilling to tell the person what you were doing. That's your test: would you be comfortable explaining it to them?

😂 Laugh line
Zappos' founder sold shoes he did not own, from shops he did not work at, on a website that was basically a photo album. Amazon later bought the company for about $1.2 billion. Meanwhile we spend four months perfecting a login screen for an app that has no users to log in.
🤯 Did you know
The Concierge MVP is the most under-used technique on this list and the most valuable, because manual work reveals the problem's true shape. When you do the job by hand for five people, you discover the exceptions, the awkward cases and the emotional bits — all the things that would have become your worst bugs if you'd automated first.
🙋 Ask the room
  1. Which rung is your project on right now? And be honest — did you skip rungs 1 through 6 entirely? (Almost everyone starts at rung 7 or 8. That's the default, and it's expensive.)
  2. What's your Rung 1 this week? Name the 10 people you'll actually talk to. Not "students" — names. (This is the single most valuable homework from today's session.)

The ladder metaphor does the teaching — lean on it. "You're trying to buy information. Buy it at the cheapest price available." Then the discipline: never climb until the rung below passes.

Zappos is the best story here and it always lands. It reframes "MVP" from "a smaller version of my app" to "any means of getting a real transaction with a real person."

Do not skip the ethics card. A sharp student will raise the fake-door objection, and if you've already addressed it you look thoughtful rather than defensive. The test — "would you be comfortable explaining what you were doing to that person?" — is a good general-purpose ethical heuristic worth giving them.

Ask #2 is the concrete homework of the entire seminar. Push for actual names, not categories. If one student names ten real people out loud, the whole room understands what "get out of the building" means. Give them 5 points.

MIN 0–2

1. Find the real problem

  • Write one How Might We — Goldilocks scope

MIN 4–6

  • Classify it: painkiller, vitamin or candy?
MIN 6–8

4. Plan to be wrong

  • Name your riskiest assumption — about a human, not the tech

🎤 Then: 3 teams present in 60 seconds each. This is the exact format:

1. "[Who] struggles to [what], and today they cope by [workaround], which costs them [number]."

2. "Our move is [one sentence]. It's better because [measurable reason]."

3. "Our riskiest assumption is [belief]. We'll test it by [cheap test]. We quit if [number]."

Pattern-matched from years of reviewing proposals

🚫 Say this instead of that

These are the exact sentences that make an experienced reviewer, interviewer or investor quietly stop paying attention — and what to say instead.

✕ "We're building an AI-powered platform for smart campuses." ✓ "Lab staff spend 6 hours a week reconciling attendance by hand. We cut it to 20 minutes."
✕ "Our target market is all students in India." ✓ "Third-year CSE students at this college during placement season — about 400 people I can physically reach."
✕ "There's no competition for this." ✓ "Today they use a WhatsApp group and a shared sheet. Here's specifically where that breaks."
✕ "We surveyed 200 students and 87% said they'd use it." ✓ "I interviewed 15 students. 11 named a specific deadline they missed last semester. 3 gave me ₹50 to be early users."
✕ "Phase 2 will add chat, payments, gamification and analytics." ✓ "We do exactly one thing completely. Here's the list of things we deliberately refuse to build."
✕ "It's like Uber, but for ___." ✓ "The job is ___. Here's why the current way of getting that job done fails."
✕ "We used React, Node, MongoDB, Docker and Kubernetes." ✓ "It's live, 40 people used it last week, and 22 came back without being reminded."
✕ "We just need funding / marketing / more time." ✓ "Here's the assumption we're testing this week, and the number at which we'd stop."
✕ "Users didn't understand the product." ✓ "We didn't understand the users. Here's what we got wrong and what we changed."
✕ "We've been working on this for 8 months." (said as proof of value) ✓ "We've run 6 experiments in 8 months. Four failed. Here's what each one taught us."

🔑 Notice the single pattern running through all ten

The left column describes what you built and what you hope. The right column describes what you learned and what you can prove.

Specificity is the tell of someone who left the building. Vagueness is the tell of someone who didn't. Experienced reviewers aren't testing your intelligence — they're scanning for evidence of contact with reality. Numbers, names, dates and quotes are that evidence. Adjectives are not.

The selfish reason to care about all of this

💼 Why this skill pays you back — whether or not you ever start a company

Most of you will not found a startup. Almost all of you will be handed a badly-specified problem by somebody senior — and how you respond to that decides your career trajectory.

🎤

In a placement interview

Two candidates describe the same project.

Candidate A: "I built a full-stack attendance system using React, Node and MongoDB with JWT authentication." → Interviewer thinks: fine, another CRUD app. Next question.

Candidate B: "I found that lab staff spend 6 hours a week reconciling attendance by hand. I sat with two of them and watched. It turned out the real problem wasn't recording attendance — it was correcting it afterwards. So I built only the correction workflow. It's used by 3 labs and saves about 4 hours a week." → Interviewer thinks: this person can be trusted with an ambiguous problem.

Identical technical work. Completely different signal. The second one demonstrates judgement — and judgement is what senior roles are actually paid for.

📈

In your first three years on the job

Every organisation is full of people who can build what they're told. They are valuable, replaceable and capped.

The person who gets promoted is the one who says: "Before I build this — who's it for, what happens today, and how will we know if it worked?"

Ask those three questions politely and consistently, and within two years people will start bringing you problems instead of instructions. That transition — from receiving tasks to being handed problems — is what a career actually is.

This is not theory. It's the difference between the engineers I've watched plateau and the ones I've watched become architects.

🤖

And in the age of AI, this is the skill

AI is getting extremely good at building the thing. Code, tests, boilerplate, documentation, even architecture drafts — the cost of execution is collapsing.

Which of today's two columns just became more valuable?

If anyone can build almost anything quickly, then deciding what deserves to exist is the scarce skill. The bottleneck has moved from "can we build it?" to "should we?"

I lead agentic AI work for a living. The hard part is never the model. It's knowing which problem is worth pointing it at.

🎓

And for your final-year project, concretely

  • Talk to 10 real users this week. Write their exact words down. Quote them in your report — panels almost never see real quotes
  • Show one killed idea and why you killed it. Demonstrating judgement beats demonstrating effort
  • Report a number that got worse, and what you did about it. Honesty reads as competence
  • Do one thing completely rather than five things partially
  • Put the problem statement on slide 1, not your architecture diagram

None of these require more technical ability than you already have. All of them are free. Most of your peers will do none of them.
